Dasara in Tulu Nadu: The Goddess Who Rides a Tiger

As I write this, it is Dasara, and somewhere down the coast a man painted like a tiger is dancing in the street to a drum. This is my attempt to write down every ritual of the festival as we keep it here — carefully, and with sources.

Most of India knows Dasara as the day good beat evil — Rama's arrow finding Ravana, or Durga finishing Mahishasura. The great royal version of it is Mysuru's, a few hundred kilometres inland: caparisoned elephants, the fierce warrior-goddess Chamundeshwari, a parade fit for the old kings.

Come down to the coast and the festival quietly changes shape. Here, in Tulu Nadu, the nine nights of Navaratri and the tenth day of Vijayadashami belong less to the sword than to the book — to Sharada, the goddess of learning, music and the arts. And the streets belong to the tiger. Ours is a Dasara of scholars and craftsmen and painted dancers, and I've always loved that the fiercest thing in it — a tiger loose in the lanes — turns out to be an act of devotion.

A different goddess, and a telling temple

The heart of the coastal Dasara is Kudla — Mangaluru, in Tulu — and specifically the Kudroli Sri Gokarnanatheshwara temple, where the nine-day festival culminates each year.[1] Through all nine days, priests install and venerate the murti of Sharada Devi alongside Mahaganapati and the Navadurgas — the nine forms of the goddess, from Shailaputri to Siddhidatri — amid continuous puja.[1]

It matters which temple this happens in. Kudroli was consecrated in 1912 by the Kerala social reformer Sree Narayana Guru, built for the Billava community — the same Tuluva community that gave us the hero-twins Koti and Chennaya — who had been barred from the other temples of Mangaluru as "untouchables."[2] Narayana Guru's creed was "one caste, one religion, one god for all." So the grandest Dasara on this coast is held, by design, in a temple founded as an act of defiance against caste exclusion. The goddess of learning, worshipped in a house built so the excluded could finally worship at all. I don't think the symbolism is accidental.

Mysuru's Dasara remembers a king's victory. Ours remembers that everyone has the right to learn. Same festival, a very different thing being celebrated.

The dolls: Gombe Habba

Before any of the temple grandeur, Dasara begins at home, on a staircase of dolls. The Gombe Habba — the doll festival — is the household face of Navaratri across Karnataka, including here.[3] Families build a tiered display, traditionally of nine steps for the nine nights, and arrange a collection of figures across them: gods and goddesses at the top, kings and sages and ordinary village life descending to the bottom.[3] Many families pick a theme — a scene from the Ramayana or Mahabharata — and build the whole arrangement around it.[3]

Then you invite people. Through the nine days, neighbours and relatives come to see each other's gombe, the women are given haldi-kumkum and a small gift, and children move from house to house cataloguing whose display is best. It is a soft, domestic, deeply social ritual — a whole festival whose main activity is visiting — and it turns the nine nights into a slow circuit of open doors.

The books we are, for once, allowed to close

If you were a student, the best part of Dasara was always this: the days the books went to the goddess. In the Saraswati Puja — Sharada and Saraswati are the same goddess of learning — books, pens, and musical instruments are placed before her, usually from the seventh day (Saptami, when the goddess is invoked) through Mahanavami.[4] And here is the rule every child loved: once they are laid before her, you may not touch them. For two or three days the textbooks are sacred, which is to say off-limits. A religiously sanctioned break from studying, blessed by the goddess of study herself. I have never encountered a more elegant piece of theology.

The day we worship our tools

On Mahanavami, the ninth day, comes the ritual I find quietly profound: Ayudha Puja, the worship of instruments. Tools, farm implements, musical instruments, vehicles, books — and now computers — are cleaned, marked with sandal paste and kumkum, garlanded, and set before the goddess.[5] For a day, the thing you work with is treated as holy.

On the coast this means the whole machinery of ordinary life stops to be honoured: the areca cutter and the plough, the auto-rickshaw with fresh flowers across its windscreen, the fishing boat, the tailor's machine, the shop's weighing scale. Nothing is too humble. The logic is beautiful and old — that your livelihood is not just yours but a gift worth thanking, and that the tool deserves a day of rest and reverence as much as its owner does.

I grew up to make my living with a laptop and a rack of servers instead of a plough. But on Ayudha Puja I still feel the pull of it — the instinct to garland the thing I build with, to thank the machine. It is the same respect for your craft, wearing different clothes.

Vijayadashami: the day to begin

The tenth day, Vijayadashami, is the auspicious one — the day traditionally held best for starting anything new, from a business to a child's education. Two rituals define it here.

The first is Banni (Shami) worship. In the afternoon or evening, families honour the Banni tree, remembering how the Pandavas hid their weapons in a Shami tree through their year in disguise and recovered them, victorious, on this day.[6] People exchange Banni leaves — offered as symbolic "gold" — as a blessing. The weapons the heroes hid away and took up again: a festival that honours both the laying-down and the picking-up of your tools.

The second is the one that shaped me without my noticing. Vidyarambha — "the beginning of knowledge" — is observed strongly in Karnataka and Kerala, and Vijayadashami is its great day.[7] A small child is formally initiated into learning: held in the lap of a parent or elder, their finger is guided to trace the first letters in a tray of rice or sand, writing them for the very first time on the most auspicious morning of the year.[7] A culture that picks one day above all others to say to its children: today you begin to learn. Everything on this blog is, in some sense, me still answering that.

At Kudroli, Vijayadashami ends in spectacle: in the evening, a great procession — the shobha yatra — sets out from the temple with the decorated goddesses, winding through the city before the murtis are carried to the temple tank and immersed, the Pushkarini taking the goddess back.[1] On the ninth day the Mangaladevi temple runs its Rathotsava, the chariot festival.[1] For a week the coast is lights and crowds and drums.

And then there are the tigers

Which brings me to the thing no account of a Tulu Nadu Dasara can leave out, and which I could not fit here without doubling the length: the Pili Vesha, the tiger dance. Troupes of young men paint their bodies as tigers and prowl the streets through Navaratri to the beat of the thaase drums, because the tiger is the goddess's mount — and dancing as her tiger is a vow paid to her.[8]

It deserves its own piece, and I gave it one: Pili Vesha: The Tiger in the Streets.

What the coast made of Dasara

Step back and look at the whole shape of it. Inland, the festival is a warrior-goddess and a king's triumph. Here, we took the same ten days and made them about something else entirely: the goddess of learning, worshipped in a temple built against caste; children beginning their letters; craftsmen honouring their tools; students granted a holy excuse to put the books down; neighbours walking door to door to look at dolls — and, loose through all of it, the goddess's tiger, danced into being by men keeping a vow.

It is a festival that worships knowledge and craft and the right to both. That is an inheritance I'm proud of, and one I keep in my own small way — I still can't start anything important on a day that isn't Vijayadashami, and I still feel the urge, every Mahanavami, to garland the machines I build with. The goddess rides a tiger: learning and ferocity, grace and power, the gentlest thing and the fiercest, on one back. I've never found a better picture of what it takes to actually make something.
