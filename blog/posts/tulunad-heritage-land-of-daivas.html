Tulunad Heritage: The Land of Daivas

I grew up in Sullia, in the foothills of the Western Ghats, at the eastern edge of a coastal world that worships spirits as neighbours. This is my attempt to write down what Tulu Nadu actually is — its gods, its rituals, its lost script — carefully, and with sources, not from memory alone.

Every culture has a way of explaining itself to outsiders in one sentence. For Tulu Nadu, the sentence people now reach for is: "that's where Kantara came from." That's not wrong — but it's like describing an ocean by one wave. The spirit worship the film showed the world is real, and it is only one thread in something far older and stranger and more alive.

I want to lay that out properly. I'm from here, but being from a place is not the same as understanding it, so I've gone back to sources — cultural archives, encyclopaedias, Unicode proposals, ethnographic writing — and cited them throughout. Where something is legend, I've called it legend. Where it's my own memory, that's mine and uncited. Everything else has a number you can follow to the bottom of the page.

Where "here" is

Tulu Nadu — ತುಳುನಾಡು, "the Tulu land" — is the strip of coast between the Arabian Sea and the Western Ghats in southwestern India. It spans the districts of Dakshina Kannada and Udupi in Karnataka and Kasaragod in Kerala.[1][2] In older tradition this whole coastal belt is called Parashurama Kshetra, land said to have been reclaimed from the sea by the sage Parashurama.[6]

My home, Sullia, sits at the inland corner of this world — a taluk of Dakshina Kannada in the foothills of the Ghats, about 850 km² of evergreen forest and river valley cut by the Kumaradhara and Payaswini rivers, arecanut gardens on every slope.[9] It's a border in the linguistic sense too: around Sullia and Puttur many of us speak Arebhashe (also called Aregannada or Gowda Kannada), a dialect of Kannada — and speakers here are typically bilingual with Tulu.[10] So I write about Tulu Nadu as someone raised on its edge, where the coast's Tulu world meets the hills — close enough to belong to it, angled enough to see it.

The language, and the script it lost

Tulu is a Dravidian language, related to Kannada, Tamil and Malayalam but distinct from all of them.[2] Today it is written in the Kannada script — but that is not the script it was born with.

Tulu's own traditional script is Tigalari (also called the Tulu script), a Southern Brahmic script descended from Grantha, in use from around the 8th–9th century CE. It was used to write Sanskrit, Kannada and Tulu.[3] When printing arrived, though, Tigalari never made the jump to type; missionaries and presses set Tulu in the Kannada script instead, and the older script slipped toward oblivion — surviving mostly in palm-leaf manuscripts.[3]

Its rescue is recent and, to me, moving. After years of campaigning, the Tulu-Tigalari script was accepted into Unicode (version 16.0, 2024), given the code-point block U+11380–U+113FF — a formal, permanent place in the digital alphabet of the world.[4][5] The Karnataka Tulu Sahitya Academy has been reintroducing both spoken Tulu and the Tigalari script in schools across the Mangalore and Udupi districts.[3]

On Tigalari: I've set the words above in the Kannada script because that is how Tulu is actually written and read today, and it renders on every device. The traditional Tigalari glyphs, though now in Unicode, still need special fonts most phones and browsers don't ship yet — so showing them here would give you empty boxes rather than letters.[4] That gap is exactly the problem the revival is trying to close.

Aliyasantana: descent through the mother

One thing that quietly shapes everything else here: much of Tulu Nadu traditionally follows Aliyasantana, a matrilineal system of inheritance and family. Property and lineage pass not from father to son but through the mother's line — classically, from a man to his sister's children (uncle to nephew/niece).[7] It doesn't apply to every community, but it has given Tuluva society a distinctive shape, and it echoes in the spirit traditions too, where family shrines and duties descend through the same lines.

Daivas and Bhuta Kola: the beating heart

Now the part outsiders find hardest to hold, because it doesn't map onto "religion" as most people mean it.

A daiva (or bhuta) is not a god in the temple sense, and not a ghost. Scholars describe them as "semi-divine spirits distinct from humans and gods."[1] Many of them are the deified dead: historical figures who "performed heroic deeds and achieved martyrdom in the past" and were, after death, raised into spirits who watch over the land.[1] Others are forces of the wild given a face — a boar, a tiger, a serpent, a mountain. They are local, tied to a village, a family, a field. And unlike temple gods worshipped daily, a daiva is invoked, called down on specific occasions.[1]

The calling-down is the ritual the world now knows as Bhuta Kola — in Tulu, bhuta = spirit and kola = play or performance.[1] It is theatre, worship, and courtroom at once, held mostly in the dry season between roughly December and May.[1]

The performance

The performer traditionally comes from specific non-Brahmin communities — the Pambada, Nalike, and Parava.[1] Through the night he becomes the vessel: layered in makeup, heavy costume and silver jewellery, wearing the ani — a towering halo-like structure that rises behind the body — with masks, headgear, and the gaggara, the heavy bronze anklets whose sound is part of the summoning.[1] Face painted, torch-lit, he does not represent the daiva. He is possessed by it.[1]

And then the spirit speaks. Once possessed, the oracle forecasts, blesses, and — this is the part I find most remarkable — mediates: resolving disputes, giving moral judgment, settling matters between villagers who have agreed to be bound by what the daiva says.[1] It is, in a real sense, the community's own justice, spoken in the spirit's voice, and open to all castes.[8]

The daivas people name

There are hundreds. A few that anyone from here grows up knowing:

  • Panjurli (ಪಂಜುರ್ಲಿ) — the divine wild boar. Legend says a boar piglet, orphaned at Mount Kailash, was raised by Parvati; when it grew destructive, Shiva sent it to earth as Panjurli, protector of forests and crops.[8] He is one of the oldest and most widely worshipped daivas across Tulu Nadu.[8]
  • Guliga — Panjurli's cosmic counterpart. If Panjurli is mercy and fertility, Guliga is justice and retribution; together they hold the balance of protection and discipline.[8] (These are the two the film Kantara put on screen.[8])
  • Koti and Chennaya — twin heroes of the Billava community, born to Deyi Baidyethi, immortalised in one of the two great long epics of the Tulu language. They died fighting, near Enmur, and are worshipped as protectors in shrines called garodi.[11]
  • Kalkuda and Kallurti — a sculptor and his sister. By legend Kalkuda was the master craftsman behind the great Bahubali (Gommateshwara) monolith at Karkala; wronged and maimed, the siblings were, after death, raised into daivas of justice.[11]
  • Bermer (a Tuluva form of Brahma), and a vast host besides — Jumadi (androgynous), Pilichamundi (in tiger form), Bobbarya, Kallurti, and many village guardians.[1]
What strikes me, every time, is that so many daivas began as people — the wronged, the heroic, the martyred — lifted by memory into something that guards the living. It is a culture that turns its dead into its protectors.

Nagaradhane: the serpents in the grove

Alongside the daivas runs an even older-feeling worship: Nagaradhane, the veneration of serpent deities.[12] Nagas are held to be guardians — of fertility, of the household, of hidden treasure — and they are honoured not in grand temples but in Nagabana, sacred groves left deliberately wild, where a carved serpent-stone sits under the trees and the forest itself is the shrine.[7] To cut such a grove is taboo. In a coast that has otherwise developed fast, these small untouched forests are, in effect, sacred ecology — conservation enforced by belief.

Aati: the month of rain and bitter medicine

If you want to feel Tulu Nadu rather than read about it, come in Aati (ಆಟಿ) — the fourth month of the traditional calendar, falling roughly in July–August, the depth of the monsoon.[13] It is the lean, streaming, grey-green month when the old year's grain runs low and the rain doesn't stop, and the traditions of Aati are all about surviving it — body and spirit.

Aati Kalenja (ಆಟಿ ಕಳೆಂಜ) is the month's wandering spirit. A single performer takes the form of the spirit Kalenja, believed to descend into the land during Aati to drive out disease — no small idea in the month when damp breeds sickness — going door to door, dancing, sprinkling a mixture of water with charcoal, turmeric and tamarind, carrying protection from house to house.[14]

And then Aati Amavasya, the new-moon day, when families do something that sounds like folklore and turns out to be pharmacology. Before sunrise they drink Paaleda Kashaya — a bitter decoction from the bark of the Paale tree (Alstonia scholaris; Saptaparni in Sanskrit, "the Devil's Tree" in English), traditionally prepared by pounding the bark with a stone.[15] It is drunk for its medicinal power over the stomach and the season's ailments, and chased with Metteda Ganji, a warming rice-and-fenugreek gruel with jaggery.[15] A monsoon medicine, taken once a year, older than any clinic here.

Aati has more to it than fits here — the "forbidden" month, its wild medicinal foods, and the spirit Kalenja who walks the villages. I gave it a piece of its own: Aati: The Month With No Gods.

Kambala and Yakshagana: the fields and the night

Two more things you cannot separate from this land. Kambala is the famous buffalo race, run by pairs of buffaloes and their runners down flooded, slushy paddy tracks — part sport, part harvest ritual, and now a fierce point of regional pride.[7]

And Yakshagana — the night-long, open-air dance-drama of gods and demons and epics, in towering painted costume and headgear, sung and drummed and declaimed until dawn.[7] Together with Bhuta Kola, Nagaradhane and Aati Kalenja, it is counted among the defining features of Tuluva culture.[7]

Why I'm writing it down

The easy mistake is to treat all this as a museum — colourful, ancient, finished. It isn't. Bhuta Kola still settles real disputes. Nagabana groves still stand because people still won't cut them. Aati kashaya is still bitter on the tongue every new moon of the rains. The script is being carried, letter by letter, back into schools and into Unicode. This is a living inheritance, and living things need tending.

I left the coast to build systems out of software. But the oldest system I know is this one — a way a small region taught itself justice, medicine, ecology and memory, and encoded it not in files but in festivals. Writing it down, with sources, is my small act of tending. There's far more than one post can hold — each daiva has its own paddana (sung epic), each village its own spirits — and I'll come back to them. If you know this world better than I do, or I've got a detail wrong, the contact links are open. Corrections are welcome; that's the whole point of sources.
